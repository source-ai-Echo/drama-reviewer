# Diagnostic Framework

Use this framework selectively. The material, format, development stage, and user's question determine which dimensions matter.

## 1. Dramatic promise

Determine what experience the story appears to promise its audience:

- What is distinctive about the premise, situation, world, or point of view?
- What emotional or dramatic question invites the audience to continue?
- Does the opening establish the kind of story that the rest delivers?
- Does the ending pay off the central promise, even if it does so unexpectedly?

Do not confuse a topic with a dramatic premise. A topic says what the story concerns; a dramatic premise implies a character, pressure, choice, or consequence.

## 2. Story engine and causality

Identify:

- protagonist or primary point-of-view character;
- active external goal;
- internal need, wound, contradiction, or avoided truth when supported by the text;
- opposing person, institution, environment, belief, or circumstance;
- stakes of failure and cost of success;
- urgency or forces preventing indefinite delay;
- central dramatic question.

Check whether the story can continue generating conflict without repeated coincidence or arbitrary obstruction. Strong progression usually follows this chain:

```text
choice -> consequence -> new pressure -> harder choice
```

Coincidence may create trouble, but repeated coincidence should not resolve decisive trouble for the protagonist.

## 3. Structure and escalation

Look for functional turns rather than mandatory page numbers or a prescribed beat sheet:

- The status quo becomes unstable.
- The protagonist commits, refuses, or becomes unable to return unchanged.
- New information or consequences alter the plan.
- Pressure narrows the available choices.
- A crisis forces a meaningful decision.
- The climax tests the story's central conflict or value.
- The ending reveals the consequences of the decisive action.

Ask whether later complications are more personal, costly, irreversible, or morally difficult than earlier ones. Repetition without changed stakes is not escalation.

For nonlinear work, distinguish intentional delayed information from confusion. The audience may lack facts while still understanding what to feel, expect, or question.

## 4. Character and arc

Evaluate characters through behavior under pressure:

- Are important actions motivated by something established or inferable?
- Does the protagonist make consequential choices rather than merely receive events?
- Are wants, fears, values, tactics, and contradictions differentiated?
- Do relationships change through action, disclosure, betrayal, sacrifice, or recognition?
- If there is an arc, is it earned through accumulated choices and consequences?
- If the character deliberately does not change, does the story make the cost or meaning of that refusal legible?

Do not require every character to have a transformational arc. Flat, tragic, circular, or disillusionment arcs may be intentional.

## 5. Scene design

For each important scene, identify:

- whose scene it is;
- what that character wants now;
- what resists the goal;
- what tactic the character uses;
- what changes by the end;
- why the next scene must happen.

A scene may justify itself through plot, character, relationship, theme, suspense, comedy, or atmosphere, but it should create a meaningful audience effect. When a scene feels slow, test for an unclear objective, weak resistance, repeated information, late conflict, or no consequential turn before recommending that it simply be shortened.

## 6. Dialogue and subtext

Check whether dialogue:

- reflects different vocabularies, rhythms, evasions, and social strategies;
- is shaped by what each speaker wants from the exchange;
- allows behavior, silence, implication, or contradiction to carry meaning;
- avoids explaining information both characters already know solely for the audience;
- changes the balance of knowledge, power, intimacy, or danger;
- remains speakable and appropriate to genre and format.

Direct dialogue is not automatically weak. Clarity can be the correct choice in comedy, procedural storytelling, children's work, heightened genres, or moments of emotional rupture.

## 7. Pacing and information

Separate several causes that are often grouped under "pacing":

- **Event pacing:** how frequently consequential events occur.
- **Emotional pacing:** whether reactions receive enough or too much space.
- **Information pacing:** when the audience learns, anticipates, or reinterprets facts.
- **Scene pacing:** how quickly the local objective, conflict, and turn develop.

Check whether questions are opened, developed, and answered at useful intervals. Mystery should create trackable curiosity rather than merely withhold basic orientation.

## 8. Genre, tone, and format

Judge the work by its intended contract:

- Does tension, humor, romance, dread, wonder, or another primary pleasure recur and escalate?
- Are tonal shifts prepared or meaningfully disruptive?
- Does the scale fit the format and likely running time?
- For short-form drama, does the story establish orientation and pressure quickly enough for its duration?
- For stage work, are entrances, exits, space, live transitions, and performability considered?

Treat production feasibility as a separate note, not proof that a dramatic choice is weak.

For serialized and short-form projects, distinguish:

- **Series engine:** the recurring pressure or conflict capable of generating multiple episodes.
- **Season or major arc:** the causal progression toward a specific irreversible confrontation or payoff.
- **Episode engine:** what a typical episode's protagonist wants, what resists it, and what changes.
- **Hook cadence:** where revelations, reversals, decisions, or new threats renew attention at the intended episode length.

When only a pitch or series bible is supplied, evaluate whether these engines are defined. Do not claim that individual episode hooks, scenes, dialogue, or minute-by-minute pacing work unless the material actually contains them.

## 9. Theme and ending

Infer theme from recurring choices, consequences, images, and relationships rather than asking characters to state a message. Ask:

- What competing values does the story dramatize?
- Does the climax force a choice between those values?
- Does the ending resolve or productively transform the central dramatic question?
- Are major setups paid off, deliberately denied, or accidentally abandoned?

## 10. Prioritization test

Before presenting a note, ask:

1. Is it supported by the supplied material?
2. Does it affect comprehension, investment, tension, emotion, or payoff?
3. Is it a root cause or only a symptom?
4. Would fixing it create downstream improvements?
5. Can the writer act on the note without abandoning the intended story?

Rank root causes and high-leverage revisions first. Use numerical scores only when the user requests them or when they clarify a comparison; scores must be accompanied by evidence.
